Anaemia — Microcytic

6 diseases
IDAIron Deficiency Anaemia
Most common anaemia worldwide

Definition

  • The most common type of anaemia
  • Low Hb due to insufficient iron for haem synthesis
  • MCV < 76 fL — microcytic, hypochromic anaemia

Aetiology

  • Blood loss: menorrhagia, GI bleeding (most common in adults)
  • Poor diet (babies/children); hookworm in the Tropics
  • Malabsorption: coeliac disease (refractory IDA)

Clinical Picture

  • Fatigue, weakness, dizziness, dyspnoea, pallor
  • Chronic IDA (now rare): koilonychia, atrophic glossitis, angular cheilosis
  • Plummer-Vinson syndrome: post-cricoid webs + dysphagia + IDA
  • IDA with no obvious source mandates GI workup (gastroscopy, colonoscopy)

Complications

  • Impaired work performance and cognitive function
  • Pica
  • Restless legs syndrome
  • Exacerbation of cardiac disease (angina, heart failure)

Management

  • Treat the underlying cause
  • Oral iron (ferrous sulfate 200 mg TDS PO); Hb rises ~10 g/L/week
  • Continue until Hb normal + 3 months to replenish stores
  • SE: nausea, constipation/diarrhoea, black stools
  • IV iron if oral route impossible (e.g. CKD on erythropoietin)
SIDSideroblastic Anaemia
Ring sideroblasts on marrow

Definition

  • Failure of haem synthesis in mitochondria → iron deposits forming a ring around nucleus
  • Diagnostic hallmark: ring sideroblasts on Prussian blue staining of bone marrow
  • May be congenital (delta-aminolaevulinate synthase-2 deficiency) or acquired
  • Characterised by ineffective erythropoiesis → ↑iron absorption → haemosiderosis

Aetiology

  • Congenital: delta-aminolaevulinate synthase-2 deficiency
  • Acquired: myelodysplasia, alcohol, lead, anti-TB drugs, chemotherapy, irradiation

Clinical Picture

  • Features of anaemia: fatigue, dyspnoea, pallor, dizziness
  • Iron overload: hepatosplenomegaly, skin hyperpigmentation, arthropathy
  • Cardiovascular: palpitations, tachycardia; heart failure if severe
  • Blood film: basophilic stippling of RBCs

Complications

  • Iron overload → haemosiderosis (endocrine, liver, heart damage)
  • Progression to myelodysplastic syndrome (if acquired)

Management

  • Supportive care; treat underlying cause
  • Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) may help (especially congenital)
  • Think of sideroblastic anaemia whenever a microcytic anaemia is not responding to iron
LEADLead Poisoning
ABC mnemonic · Basophilic stippling

Definition

  • Defective ferrochelatase and ALA dehydratase → impaired haem synthesis
  • Consider alongside acute intermittent porphyria when abdominal pain + neurological signs
  • ABC mnemonic: Abdominal pain, Blue gum lines, Constipation, Decreased motor function, Emotional changes, Fatigue

Aetiology

  • Occupational exposure: paint, batteries, plumbing
  • Environmental: contaminated water, soil, leaded petrol (historical)
  • Pica (children ingesting lead-based paint)

Clinical Picture

  • Microcytic anaemia with basophilic stippling and clover-leaf morphology on blood film
  • Abdominal pain, constipation
  • Blue lines on gum margin (20% adults, rare in children)
  • Peripheral neuropathy, neuropsychiatric features
  • Raised serum/urine δ-ALA (may mimic acute intermittent porphyria)

Complications

  • Encephalopathy (especially in children)
  • Peripheral neuropathy, wrist drop
  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Developmental delay in children

Management

  • Chelation therapy: DMSA (dimercaptosuccinic acid) first-line
  • Alternatives: D-penicillamine, EDTA, dimercaprol
  • Blood lead level >10 mcg/dL considered significant
  • Remove from source of exposure
B-MAJBeta-Thalassaemia Major
Cooley's anaemia · AR

Definition

  • Autosomal recessive deficiency of beta-globin chain synthesis (chromosome 11)
  • Common in Mediterranean, African, Southeast Asian populations
  • Free alpha chain accumulation → toxic inclusions → haemolysis + ineffective erythropoiesis

Aetiology

  • Mutations in beta-globin gene on chromosome 11
  • Autosomal recessive inheritance

Clinical Picture

  • Presentation in 1st year of life: failure to thrive, hepatosplenomegaly
  • Thalassaemic facies: bone expansion from extramedullary haemopoiesis
  • Skull X-ray: 'hair on end' appearance of bony trabeculation
  • Microcytic, hypochromic RBCs with target cells on blood film
  • Hb electrophoresis: low HbA, ↑HbF and HbA2

Complications

  • Iron overload from transfusions → endocrine, liver, pancreatic, myocardial damage
  • Extramedullary haemopoiesis → bone deformities, facial changes
  • Gallstones, splenomegaly
  • Infection risk (post-splenectomy)

Management

  • Regular transfusions to keep Hb >100 g/L (every 4–6 weeks)
  • Folic acid supplements long-term
  • Iron chelation: desferrioxamine (parenteral) or deferiprone (oral) + ascorbic acid 200 mg/day
  • Splenectomy if transfusion requirements increase (delay until >6 yrs old)
  • Bone marrow transplant in young patients with HLA-matched sibling (3-yr mortality <5% if good condition)
B-TRTBeta-Thalassaemia Trait
Mild microcytosis · HbA2 ↑

Definition

  • Mild hypochromic, microcytic anaemia — one mutated beta-globin gene
  • Usually asymptomatic; microcytosis disproportionate to degree of anaemia
  • Must distinguish from iron deficiency

Aetiology

  • Single gene mutation — autosomal recessive carrier

Clinical Picture

  • Mild microcytosis; HbA2 raised (>3.5%)
  • Normal serum ferritin and iron stores
  • RDW usually normal (vs. ↑RDW in IDA)
  • Hb electrophoresis: raised HbA2 and often raised HbF

Complications

  • Often confused with iron deficiency anaemia
  • Iron should NOT be given unless proven coincidental iron deficiency

Management

  • No treatment needed (asymptomatic)
  • Genetic counselling for carriers
  • Only give iron if proven coincidental iron deficiency
α-THALAlpha-Thalassaemia
4 gene deletions · Hydrops fetalis

Definition

  • Deficiency of alpha chains due to deletion of alpha-globin genes on chromosome 16
  • 2 alpha-globin genes per chromosome 16 (4 total per cell)
  • Severity depends on number of affected alleles

Aetiology

  • Gene deletions on chromosome 16 (4 genes total)
  • 1–2 genes deleted: carrier /silent
  • 3 genes deleted: HbH disease
  • 4 genes deleted: hydrops fetalis (Bart's) — death in utero

Clinical Picture

  • 1–2 genes: asymptomatic or mild microcytosis, normal Hb
  • 3 genes (HbH disease): microcytic anaemia, splenomegaly
  • 4 genes: Bart's hydrops fetalis — death in utero

Complications

  • HbH disease: chronic haemolytic anaemia, splenomegaly, gallstones
  • 4-gene deletion: intrauterine death

Management

  • 1–2 genes: no treatment
  • HbH disease: supportive, folate, occasional transfusion, splenectomy if severe
  • 4 genes: intrauterine transfusion (experimental) or termination
  • Genetic counselling; prenatal diagnosis available

Anaemia — Macrocytic

7 diseases
FOLFolate Deficiency
Body stores 4 months

Definition

  • Folate found in green vegetables, nuts, yeast, liver; synthesised by gut bacteria
  • MCV >96 fL — megaloblastic macrocytic anaemia
  • Body stores last ~4 months; absorbed in duodenum/proximal jejunum
  • Maternal deficiency → foetal neural tube defects

Aetiology

  • Poor diet: poverty, alcoholics, elderly
  • Increased demand: pregnancy, haemolysis, malignancy, inflammatory disease, dialysis
  • Malabsorption: coeliac disease, tropical sprue
  • Drugs: alcohol, anti-epileptics (phenytoin, valproate), methotrexate, trimethoprim

Clinical Picture

  • Macrocytic anaemia with megaloblasts on blood film
  • General anaemia symptoms: fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea
  • May present with glossitis

Complications

  • Neural tube defects in foetus (spina bifida) if maternal deficiency in early pregnancy
  • May precipitate or worsen subacute combined degeneration if B12 co-deficiency untreated

Management

  • Treat underlying cause
  • Folic acid 5 mg/day PO for 4 months
  • Never give folate alone without B12 unless B12 known to be normal — may worsen subacute combined degeneration
  • Pregnancy prophylaxis: folic acid 400 µg/day from conception to ≥12 weeks
B12B12 Deficiency & Pernicious Anaemia
Autoimmune · Body stores 4 years

Definition

  • Vitamin B12 found in meat, fish, dairy (NOT plants); body stores ~4 years
  • B12 binds intrinsic factor in stomach → absorbed in terminal ileum
  • B12 deficiency → impaired DNA synthesis → megaloblastic anaemia
  • Pernicious anaemia: autoimmune destruction of gastric mucosa → lack of intrinsic factor
  • Neurological signs of B12 deficiency can occur without anaemia

Aetiology

  • Dietary: vegans
  • Malabsorption — stomach: pernicious anaemia, post-gastrectomy
  • Malabsorption — terminal ileum: Crohn's, ileal resection, bacterial overgrowth, tropical sprue, Diphyllobothrium tapeworm
  • Drugs: metformin, acid-suppressors (ranitidine, PPIs)
  • Associated autoimmune diseases: thyroid disease, T1DM, Addison's, vitiligo, RA
  • More common in females (F:M = 1.6:1), blood group A, middle/old age

Clinical Picture

  • Macrocytic anaemia; hypersegmented polymorphs on blood film
  • Peripheral neuropathy: 'pins and needles', numbness — symmetrical, legs > arms
  • Subacute combined degeneration: dorsal column loss (joint position, vibration) + corticospinal tract loss
  • Neuropsychiatric: memory loss, poor concentration, confusion, depression
  • 'Lemon tinge' skin: mild jaundice + pallor
  • Atrophic glossitis → sore tongue

Complications

  • Subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord: may be irreversible if untreated
  • Increased risk of gastric cancer (pernicious anaemia)
  • Pancytopenia (low WCC and platelets may be seen)

Management

  • No neurological features: IM hydroxocobalamin 1 mg 3×/week for 2 weeks, then 3-monthly
  • More frequent doses if neurological features present
  • Always treat B12 before folate to avoid precipitating subacute combined degeneration
  • Anti-intrinsic factor antibodies: sensitivity 50%, specificity 95–100%
  • Anti-parietal cell antibodies in 90% but low specificity
  • Monitoring: Hb rises ~10 g/L/week; WCC and platelets normalise in 1 week
  • Peripheral neuropathy improves in 3–6 months; cord signs rarely improve
SCDSubacute Combined Degeneration
B12 deficiency · Dorsal + lateral columns

Definition

  • Due to vitamin B12 deficiency → demyelination of:
  • Dorsal columns (proprioception, vibration), lateral corticospinal tracts (motor), spinocerebellar tracts
  • Nitrous oxide inhalation can also cause B12 deficiency → SCD

Aetiology

  • Vitamin B12 deficiency (any cause — see B12/Pernicious Anaemia)
  • Recreational nitrous oxide abuse
  • Pernicious anaemia (most common cause)

Clinical Picture

  • Insidious (subacute) onset with peripheral neuropathy
  • Dorsal column loss: distal tingling/burning, impaired proprioception and vibration → ataxia
  • Corticospinal tract: muscle weakness, hyperreflexia, spasticity, UMN signs in legs
  • Spinocerebellar: sensory ataxia, positive Romberg's sign
  • Classical triad: extensor plantars (UMN) + absent knee jerks (LMN) + absent ankle jerks (LMN)
  • Pain and temperature remain intact (spinothalamic tracts spared)
  • May present with falls at night (ataxia + reduced vision)

Complications

  • Irreversible cord damage if not treated promptly
  • Progression to spastic paraplegia
  • Combined with megaloblastic anaemia (but can occur without anaemia)

Management

  • Urgent IM hydroxocobalamin replacement
  • Prognosis: treat as soon as possible after symptom onset
  • Peripheral neuropathy may improve; cord signs often permanent
ALCAlcoholic Macrocytosis
Direct toxic effect on marrow

Definition

  • Macrocytosis (MCV >100) without anaemia in chronic alcohol excess; direct toxic effect on marrow precursors

Aetiology

  • Chronic alcohol excess; liver disease alters lipid membranes of RBCs

Clinical Picture

  • Often asymptomatic; macrocytosis found on routine FBC; may have signs of liver disease; raised GGT, AST > ALT

Complications

  • May coexist with other causes (folate deficiency from poor diet, GI bleeding causing IDA)

Management

  • Abstinence from alcohol; macrocytosis reverses within 2-4 months; check folate and B12 levels; treat deficiencies if present
DRGDrug-Induced Macrocytosis
DNA synthesis interference

Definition

  • Macrocytosis caused by medications that interfere with DNA synthesis or folate metabolism

Aetiology

  • Antifolates: methotrexate, trimethoprim, pyrimethamine; DNA synthesis: hydroxyurea, azathioprine, 5-FU, 6-mercaptopurine; Others: phenytoin, valproate

Clinical Picture

  • Often asymptomatic macrocytosis on routine bloods; may develop pancytopenia with high doses; megaloblastic changes on marrow

Complications

  • Pancytopenia if severe; megaloblastic anaemia; may mask B12 deficiency

Management

  • Withdraw or reduce offending drug; folate supplementation (caution with MTX - may reduce efficacy); monitor FBC
LIVLiver Disease Macrocytosis
Target cells + acanthocytes

Definition

  • Macrocytosis secondary to chronic liver disease; target cells and acanthocytes often also present

Aetiology

  • Alcohol excess (most common); chronic hepatitis B/C; cirrhosis of any cause; altered cholesterol metabolism in RBC membrane

Clinical Picture

  • Often asymptomatic macrocytosis; stigmata of chronic liver disease; target cells, acanthocytes, spur cells on film; usually NO anaemia or mild

Complications

  • Underlying liver disease complications; may have coexisting sideroblastic anaemia; hypersplenism in portal hypertension

Management

  • Treat underlying liver disease; folate and B12 supplementation if deficient; avoid alcohol; monitor FBC
HPTHypothyroidism Macrocytosis
Shared autoimmunity with PA

Definition

  • Mild macrocytosis associated with hypothyroidism; due to reduced metabolic rate affecting marrow

Aetiology

  • Hypothyroidism (autoimmune thyroiditis most common); may also cause normocytic anaemia

Clinical Picture

  • Mild macrocytosis on FBC; features of hypothyroidism (fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, bradycardia); often coexists with pernicious anaemia (shared autoimmunity)

Complications

  • If B12 deficiency coexists (pernicious anaemia) -neurological complications; associated with other autoimmune conditions (Addison's, vitiligo)

Management

  • Thyroid hormone replacement (levothyroxine); check B12 and folate levels; macrocytosis usually mild and resolves with treatment

Haemolytic Anaemia — Acquired

6 diseases
W-AIHAWarm AIHA
IgG · Extravascular · 37°C

Definition

  • Most common type of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia
  • Antibody is IgG; causes haemolysis best at body temperature (37°C)
  • Haemolysis is extravascular (mainly spleen)
  • Direct Coombs test (DAT) positive

Aetiology

  • Idiopathic (most common)
  • Autoimmune disease: SLE
  • Neoplasia: lymphoma, CLL
  • Drugs: methyldopa

Clinical Picture

  • Anaemia symptoms: fatigue, pallor, dyspnoea
  • Jaundice, splenomegaly
  • Spherocytes and reticulocytes on blood film
  • Low haptoglobin, ↑LDH, ↑indirect bilirubin, reticulocytosis

Complications

  • Severe anaemia
  • Thromboembolism

Management

  • Treat any underlying disorder
  • Steroids first-line (+/-rituximab)
C-AIHACold AIHA
IgM · Intravascular · 4°C

Definition

  • Antibody is IgM; causes haemolysis best at 4 degrees C
  • Haemolysis is intravascular (complement-mediated)
  • Direct Coombs test positive

Aetiology

  • Neoplasia: lymphoma
  • Infections: Mycoplasma, EBV
  • SLE (rare; can cause mixed-type AIHA)

Clinical Picture

  • Symptoms of anaemia + Raynaud's, acrocyanosis
  • Worse in cold weather
  • Responds less well to steroids

Complications

  • Cold-induced circulatory symptoms
  • Less steroid-responsive than warm AIHA

Management

  • Treat underlying cause
  • Supportive care; keep warm
  • Steroids have limited effectiveness
PCHParoxysmal Cold Haemoglobinuria
Donath-Landsteiner antibodies

Definition

  • Caused by Donath–Landsteiner antibodies (IgG) that bind RBCs in the cold
  • Self-limiting complement-mediated haemolysis on rewarming
  • Direct Coombs test negative

Aetiology

  • Post-viral (children): acute, non-recurrent
  • Associated with syphilis (tertiary), haematological malignancies (adults)
  • Chronic relapsing episodes in adults

Clinical Picture

  • Acute haemoglobinuria after cold exposure
  • Children: acute post-infectious event
  • Adults: associated with syphilis or malignancy

Complications

  • Acute kidney injury from haemoglobinuria

Management

  • Supportive care; self-limiting in children
  • Treat underlying cause (syphilis, malignancy)
MAHAMicroangiopathic HA
Schistocytes · Intravascular

Definition

  • Mechanical disruption of RBCs in circulation → intravascular haemolysis
  • Blood film: schistocytes (fragmented RBCs)
  • Coombs-negative haemolysis

Aetiology

  • HUS (haemolytic-uraemic syndrome)
  • TTP (thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura)
  • DIC
  • Pre-eclampsia /eclampsia
  • Malignant hypertension, prosthetic heart valves

Clinical Picture

  • Schistocytes on blood film — key finding
  • Anaemia, thrombocytopenia, ↑LDH, low haptoglobin
  • Features of underlying cause

Complications

  • Organ damage from microvascular thrombosis
  • AKI (in HUS)
  • Neurological signs (in TTP)

Management

  • Treat the underlying disease
  • Supportive: transfusion, plasma exchange if indicated
PNHParoxysmal Nocturnal Haemoglobinuria
GPI anchor deficiency · CD55/CD59

Definition

  • Acquired disorder → increased sensitivity of cell membranes to complement
  • Lack of GPI anchor (glycosyl-phosphatidylinositol) → surface proteins (DAF, CD59) not bound
  • Mainly intravascular haemolysis; patients prone to venous thrombosis
  • Flow cytometry to detect low CD55/CD59 has replaced Ham's test as gold standard

Aetiology

  • Acquired mutation in PIG-A gene (X-linked)
  • Affects haematopoietic stem cell → RBCs, WBCs, platelets all affected
  • Can develop from aplastic anaemia

Clinical Picture

  • Haemolytic anaemia with dark-coloured urine in the morning (haemoglobinuria)
  • Pancytopenia may be present
  • Venous thrombosis e.g. Budd-Chiari syndrome
  • Aplastic anaemia may develop

Complications

  • Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis)
  • Thrombosis at unusual sites
  • Progression to aplastic anaemia
  • Chronic kidney disease from intravascular haemolysis

Management

  • Blood product replacement; anticoagulation
  • Eculizumab — monoclonal antibody against C5, reduces intravascular haemolysis
  • Stem cell transplantation (curative)
  • Flow cytometry for CD59/CD55 (replaced Ham's test)
DIHADrug-Induced Immune HA
Hapten / Autoantibody / Immune complex

Definition

  • Haemolytic anaemia caused by drug-dependent antibodies acting against RBC membrane

Aetiology

  • Hapten mechanism: penicillin, cephalosporins (drug binds RBC, IgG antibodies); Methyldopa type (true autoantibody, persists after drug stopped); Immune complex: quinidine, stibophen (complement activation)

Clinical Picture

  • Penicillin type: mild HA, +ve DAT (IgG); Methyldopa type: insidious onset, +ve DAT in 15% (only 1% get HA); Quinidine type: acute severe HA, intravascular, +ve DAT (C3d)

Complications

  • Acute kidney injury (especially immune complex type); severe anaemia; haemoglobinuria

Management

  • Stop offending drug; supportive care; steroids for severe cases; penicillin-type: usually resolves 1-2 weeks after stopping

Haemolytic Anaemia — Hereditary

5 diseases
G6PDG6PD Deficiency
X-linked · Heinz bodies · Fava beans

Definition

  • Most common RBC enzyme defect
  • X-linked recessive — males affected; common in Mediterranean and African descent
  • G6PD → NADPH → reduced glutathione (protects RBCs from oxidative damage)
  • ↓G6PD → ↓NADPH → ↓glutathione → ↑oxidative stress → haemolysis

Aetiology

  • X-linked recessive
  • Precipitated by: infections, drugs (primaquine, ciprofloxacin, sulphonamides), fava (broad) beans
  • Safe drugs: penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides, tetracyclines, trimethoprim

Clinical Picture

  • Neonatal jaundice; intravascular haemolysis
  • Gallstones; splenomegaly
  • Heinz bodies on blood film (denatured haemoglobin)
  • Bite cells and blister cells
  • Diagnosis: G6PD enzyme assay (check ~3 months post-crisis to avoid false negative)

Complications

  • Acute haemolytic crisis
  • Kernicterus (neonatal)
  • Gallstones

Management

  • Avoid precipitating drugs and fava beans
  • Supportive during crisis; transfusion if severe
  • Screening before prescribing: primaquine, sulphonamides, sulphonylureas, ciprofloxacin
PKPyruvate Kinase Deficiency
AR · ↓ATP · 2nd most common enzyme defect

Definition

  • Autosomal recessive enzyme deficiency
  • ↓ATP production → ↓RBC survival → haemolytic anaemia
  • 2nd most common RBC enzyme defect after G6PD

Aetiology

  • Autosomal recessive
  • Mutation in PKLR gene encoding pyruvate kinase

Clinical Picture

  • Homozygotes: neonatal jaundice, haemolysis with splenomegaly ± jaundice
  • Variable severity
  • Diagnosis: enzyme assay

Complications

  • Gallstones, splenomegaly
  • Aplastic crisis with parvovirus infection

Management

  • Often no treatment needed
  • Folate supplementation
  • Splenectomy may help in severe cases
HSHereditary Spherocytosis
AD · Most common hereditary HA (N. European)

Definition

  • Most common hereditary haemolytic anaemia in people of Northern European descent
  • Autosomal dominant defect of RBC cytoskeleton (spectrin, ankyrin, band 3, protein 4.2)
  • Biconcave disc → sphere-shaped RBCs → destroyed by spleen
  • MCHC elevated — key lab finding

Aetiology

  • Autosomal dominant (most common)
  • Defect in RBC membrane proteins (spectrin most common)

Clinical Picture

  • Failure to thrive, jaundice, gallstones, splenomegaly
  • Aplastic crisis precipitated by parvovirus infection
  • Blood film: spherocytes (round, lack central pallor)
  • Diagnosis: spherocytes + raised MCHC + reticulocytosis = typical; if equivocal use EMA binding test / cryohaemolysis test

Complications

  • Aplastic crisis (parvovirus)
  • Gallstones (pigmented)
  • Chronic haemolysis

Management

  • Acute crisis: supportive; transfusion if necessary
  • Folate replacement
  • Splenectomy (long-term definitive treatment)
